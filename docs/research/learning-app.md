# P-Chess as a learning app: competitors, methods, curriculum, data, licences

Research pass, 2026-11-07. Builds on [chess-learning.md](chess-learning.md) (learning-science papers); does not repeat it.
Pitch under test: *"I don't know what I'm doing, but I'm getting better just by following this."*
Tags: **[primary]** official site, docs, repo, LICENSE, publisher excerpt, or the author's own words; **[secondary]** review, retailer, blog, or search-engine summary of a page I could not open; **[anecdote]** forum or user report. Prices and features are as of today and change often.

## TL;DR

- **Nothing on the market is that exact combination, but every piece of it exists somewhere.** Noctie already has a human-like bot, play-outs from custom positions, flashcard puzzles from your own mistakes, and 100+ lessons (€14/month or €8/month yearly). ChessDojo has a staged curriculum by 100-point rating band with "sparring positions". maiachess.com has free opening and endgame drills against Maia-3. Aimchess and Chessable turn your own mistakes into lessons and puzzles. What none of them does is put a **rating-band curriculum and its practice engine (solve, play out against Maia, pass test, routing from your own games) in one free offline app**. That combination is P-Chess's only real novelty.
- **Practitioners agree on the ingredients, not the doses**: play slow games and analyse them yourself before the engine does (Dojo, Botvinnik, Heisman), play out set positions (Dvoretsky, Dojo), drill easy frequent tactics (Heisman, Woodpecker, de la Maza, Aagaard), and stage endgames by rating (Silman). They disagree on tactics-only programmes, on spacing schedules, and on how hard the drills should be.
- **Data is easy**: Lichess puzzles, games and evaluations are **CC0** (6.19M tagged, rated puzzles). **Code is the catch**: the lichess-puzzler theme tagger is **AGPL-3.0** Python, and Maia-3 is **AGPL-3.0** PyTorch. P-Chess has **no licence file**. Porting the tagger into it would force a licence decision.
- **First slice: one rook-endgame unit (Lucena and Philidor).** Own mistakes can be routed to it by material alone, with no tagger. Play-outs against Maia are scored exactly by tablebase, the pass test is objective, and the puzzle supply is CC0.

**Already done well elsewhere:** puzzles from your own mistakes (Noctie, Chessable, Aimchess, Lichess), play-outs from custom positions against a human-like bot (Noctie, maiachess.com), opening drills against a human-like bot (Noctie, maiachess.com), repertoire spaced repetition (Chessbook, Chessable), a rating-band curriculum (Dojo, and Silman's book), and "calculate the whole line, including the opponent's defences" testing (Dojo Tactics Tests, [primary](https://chessdojo.club/blog/tactics-test)).

**What would be novel:** (1) the Dojo-style staged curriculum *executed inside the app*: Dojo hands you a task list pointing to books and to human sparring, while P-Chess would serve the exercise itself; (2) one unit bundling solving + play-out against Maia + an objective pass test, i.e. gated mastery; (3) guided review that *requires your own annotation before the engine*, then routes your mistakes to units. I found no product that enforces this; Lichess, Chess.com and Aimchess all show the engine first, and Noctie lets you annotate but appears not to require it. (4) Free, offline and local, with no account. The closest single competitor to the pitch is **Noctie minus stages**. Expect users to compare against it.

## 2. Practitioner methods

**Dvoretsky** [primary]. In *Chess Lessons* he names "the specific forms of training that I routinely use during my lessons: analysis of games in the form of solving a string of consecutive tasks and **playing out of certain specially selected positions**", and the book has a whole part titled "Playing-out" ([Russell Enterprises excerpt](https://shop.chess-tigers.de/cdn/shop/files/lessons_excerpt.pdf)). Positions are chosen to be "significant": common, with a clear route to a win or a draw ([secondary](https://reviewchessbooks.blogspot.com/2020/03/review-chess-training-books-tips-for.html)). *Reflections on My Profession* has chapters titled "Solve for Yourself!" and "Captivated by the Computer's 'First Line'" ([excerpt](https://www.newinchess.com/media/wysiwyg/product_pdf/2794.pdf), table of contents only read).

**Yusupov** [primary: [Quality Chess, archived](https://web.archive.org/web/2026/https://qualitychess.co.uk/?p=17352)]: nine volumes, "three series with three levels" (Fundamentals / Beyond the Basics / Mastery × Build Up / Boost / Evolution), each lesson "tested by a selection of puzzles", topics "spread evenly over the nine volumes". [secondary:] Each book has 24 chapters, every lesson followed by test puzzles, and tactics, strategy, endgame, calculation and openings spread evenly across all nine ([retailer](https://www.simonandschuster.ca/series/Yusupovs-Chess-School); [Silman review](https://www.jeremysilman.com/book-review/boost-your-chess-1)). Rough audience ~1000–2200, by reviewers' estimates. *Shape worth copying:* every stage mixes all areas instead of running "a tactics stage" and then "an endgame stage".

**Steps Method** [primary, but the page served carried a "TESTOMGEVING" (test-environment) banner]: "six steps. Each step has a manual… and workbooks with exercises", used since 1987, "from beginner to a high level (top 700 of the Netherlands)" ([stappenmethode.nl](https://www.stappenmethode.nl/en/)). Step 6's workbook has 14 chapter-matched sections plus 1,320 mixed positions ([secondary](https://newinchess.com/learning-chess-workbook-step-6)). *Shape:* massed themed exercises first, then a large **mixed** block, which is interleaving in practice. One reviewer says the books were not written for self-study ([anecdote](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/12063907)).

**Aagaard.** On Perpetual Chess ep. 494 (Aug 2026) he says "many club players should spend more time solving **simple tactics** rather than difficult calculation exercises", and he discusses how to categorise your mistakes "without overcomplicating" it ([show notes, primary](https://www.perpetualchesspod.com/new-blog/2026/8/11/ep-494-gm-jacob-aagaard-on-how-to-improve-calculation-amp-learn-from-your-mistakesnbsp)). *GM Preparation: Calculation* has chapters on candidate moves, combinational vision, prophylaxis, comparison, elimination, intermediate moves, imagination and traps, plus 75 "difficult positions" ([secondary review](https://www.jeremysilman.com/?p=2672)). I could not verify a "solve like a game" rule in his own words.

**Kotov** (*Think Like a Grandmaster*): candidate moves, then examine each branch once, then compare the end positions. "Kotov Syndrome" names the failure of spending the time on lines you then don't play ([secondary](https://chess.com/terms/kotov-syndrome-chess)). Tisdall and Nunn criticise the tree as impractical while keeping candidate moves ([secondary](https://www.chess.com/blog/UltimateDegree/master-calculation-by-aagard), [anecdote](https://www.chess.com/forum/view/general/kotovs-tree-of-analysis)).

**Heisman** [primary]. He separates thought-process errors from content errors and lists named process errors: playing too fast or too slow for the situation, Hope Chess, the Quiescence Error, the Retained Image Error, assuming the opponent's weaker reply, not recognising critical positions, "Look Wide Before You Look Deep", Kotov Syndrome ([blog](https://www.chess.com/blog/danheisman/types-of-chess-thought-process-errors)). Tactics: "repetitious tactical puzzles, starting with the easiest ones that occur frequently… (e.g. The Woodpecker Method or de la Maza's Seven Circles)". Games: "include some regular games at time controls of **30 5 or slower**", because 15+10 leaves ~30 s a move, too little to practise analysis ([blog](https://www.chess.com/blog/danheisman/the-key-to-chess-improvement-practice)). *Is Your Move Safe?* (2016) is his blunder-check book ([secondary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Heisman)). **His error list is a ready-made tag vocabulary for the guided review, covering the "why" that motif tags miss.**

**de la Maza / Woodpecker.** de la Maza's "Seven Circles": one set of puzzles solved seven times in shrinking time windows; he reported his gain as "400 points in 400 days" (ChessCafe articles, not read; [secondary](https://www.chess.com/article/the-michael-de-la-maza-story)). *The Woodpecker Method* (Smith & Tikkanen, Quality Chess 2018): ~1,128 puzzles, re-solved in cycles that halve in time; Tikkanen made three GM norms in seven weeks after training this way ([secondary](https://chesstempo.com/chess-books/the-woodpecker-method/book/183); [anecdote: a cycle log](https://chesschatter.substack.com/p/135-hours-of-the-woodpecker-method)).

**Silman, *Complete Endgame Course*** [secondary]: nine parts organised by USCF class, from unrated–999 (basic mates, stalemate) up to Expert and Master plus a "pure pleasure" part. His advice is to study up to your class, or one part above it ([review](https://grandpatzerchess.blogspot.com/2007/03/silmans-complete-endgame-course-review.html)). The reviewer notes Silman leaves pawn-ending key squares until Class A, and quotes Heisman losing a drawable Philidor at ~2100 USCF because he had never learnt it.

**Botvinnik** [secondary]: deep analysis of his own games, *published* to invite criticism, plus a narrow repertoire and training games ([Newport](https://calnewport.com/mikhail-botvinnik-and-the-invention-of-modern-chess-training/); [chess.com](https://www.chess.com/article/view/how-to-annotate-your-games-for-chess-improvers)). His own text exists as *The Method of Preparing for Competition* (tr. Ciobanu; [publisher synopsis](https://test.blackwells.co.uk/bookshop/product/The-Method-of-Preparing-for-Competition-by-Victor-Ciobanu-translator-Victor-Ciobanu-contributions-Mikhail-Moiseyevich-Botvinnik-author/9781686257698), not read).

**ChessDojo (read closely)** [primary unless marked]:
- Built by GM Kraai and IMs Pruess and Kavutskiy to answer "is it possible to create a universal training program?" The goal is "as many students as possible efficiently improve… as measured by their rating performance" ([philosophy](https://www.chess.com/blog/ChessDojo/the-real-philosophy-of-the-dojo-training-program)).
- **Two pillars:** (1) play classical games, study them, repeat. (2) **Sparring**, "playing out segments of games, repeatedly, from certain set positions", used for openings, endgame theory, positional endgames and middlegames. Plus "sweat work", "don't ask the engine for answers", and plus/equal/minus partners for accountability (same post).
- **Structure:** "twenty three 100-elo-point chunks"; tasks are "self-paced and un-ordered"; categories Games+Analysis / Tactics / Middlegames+Strategy / Endgame / Opening / Graduation ([cohort list](https://github.com/jackstenglein/chess-dojo/blob/main/common/src/database/cohort.ts), [categories](https://github.com/jackstenglein/chess-dojo/blob/main/common/src/database/requirement.ts)). Graduation comes from rating gain, not from finishing the tasks: the FAQ says that if you reach the next band with boxes unchecked, you move on to the new requirements ([launch FAQ](https://www.chess.com/blog/ChessDojo/launching-the-dojo-training-program-0-2400)). Since 2022 the rating basis moved from FIDE to a Dojo rating translator ([secondary](https://chessdojo.club/blog/new-ratings)).
- **Rating table (2022):** FIDE 1400–1500 ≈ Chess.com rapid 1600–1800 ≈ Lichess classical 1800–2000 (launch post). Bruno would sit around Dojo 1400–1500.
- **Tactics Tests:** 6–12 hand-picked problems in 30–60 min. Some have no flashy solution ("any decent move" accepted), you must enter the opponent's replies and key defences too, and scoring counts correct moves ([tactics-test post](https://chessdojo.club/blog/tactics-test)). This is a worked-out "calculate, then commit" format.
- **Could not verify:** the actual per-cohort task lists. They sit behind login; the API needs a JWT ([serverless.yml](https://github.com/jackstenglein/chess-dojo/blob/main/backend/requirement/serverless.yml)) and the public Google sheet needs sign-in.

**Perpetual Chess** (Ben Johnson): the "Adult Improver Series" of amateur interviews since 2018 ([primary](https://www.perpetualchesspod.com/adultimprover)), and the book *Perpetual Chess Improvement* (NIC 2023). It is a collection of anecdotes, not a programme.

### Where they disagree (with each other or with chess-learning.md)

| Issue | Positions |
|---|---|
| Tactics-only vs balanced | de la Maza: tactics drilling only. Silman and Nunn criticised that ([secondary](https://www.chess.com/article/the-michael-de-la-maza-story)); Yusupov, Steps and Dojo mix all areas at every level. |
| Spacing schedule | Woodpecker repeats the same set in *shrinking* gaps (~4→2→1 weeks) ([secondary critique](https://lichess.org/@/CheckRaiseMate/blog/the-woodpecker-method/m4cW82R0)). Research says the best gap *grows* with how long you need to remember (Cepeda 2006, in chess-learning.md). Repeating one set also risks learning the positions rather than the patterns (chess-learning.md §1). |
| Hard vs easy drills | Aagaard (2026): simple tactics for club players. Dvoretsky and *GM Prep* rely on hard calculation positions. |
| Time control | Dojo: classical only, "No" to faster games. Heisman: 30+5 as the minimum. Research: blitz rating tracks overall skill (Burns 2004) and extra tournament play adds little beyond study (Burgoyne 2019, ΔR² = .006). None of this backs "classical only" as a causal claim. |
| Engine timing | Dojo: analyse alone, no engine answers. chess-learning.md: attempt, then *immediate* feedback, because the benefit of the attempt vanished when feedback was delayed (Grimaldi & Karpicke 2012). Compatible if the engine comes right after your own annotation, not days later. |
| Kotov's tree | Kotov: a full tree. Tisdall, Nunn and Heisman: candidates yes, exhaustive tree no. |
| When to learn Lucena/Philidor | Silman stages rook endings by class; Heisman argues for learning them earlier (quoted in a [review](https://grandpatzerchess.blogspot.com/2007/03/silmans-complete-endgame-course-review.html)). |

## 3. Curriculum skeleton (1200 → 2200)

Scale: **Lichess rating**, the scale Maia is trained on (Maia-1 bins 1100–1900; Maia-3 600–2600 Lichess blitz). Bruno ≈ Lichess 1900 ≈ Chess.com rapid 1600. Ordering follows Silman's class staging and Lichess Practice's grouping; the per-stage mix of areas follows Yusupov and Dojo; the theme names are the lichess-puzzler tags, so that material can be queried. **The placement is my proposal, not a citation.** Each stage: tactics + calculation + endgame + strategy + openings + practical, as in Dojo's categories.

| Stage | Tactics (puzzle tags) | Calculation | Endgames | Strategy / structures | Openings | Practical |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1: 1200–1500** | `hangingPiece`, `fork`, `pin`, `skewer`, `discoveredAttack`, `doubleCheck`, `backRankMate`, `mateIn1/2`, `short` | Checks, captures and threats on every move; Heisman's "is it safe?" | Basic mates (Q, R), K+P vs K: key squares, opposition, rule of the square (Lichess Practice) | Development, king safety, piece activity | Play your repertoire's first ~6–8 moves; principles | Slow games ≥30+5 (Heisman); blunder check |
| **2: 1500–1800** | `deflection`, `attraction`, `overloading`, `capturingDefender`, `intermezzo`, `trappedPiece`, `xRayAttack`, `clearance`, named mates, `mateIn3`, `long` | "Calculate, then commit": enter the line *including the defender's replies* (Dojo test format) | **Lucena, Philidor**, rook vs pawn, passive rook vs 7th-rank rook pawn (Lichess Practice "Rook endgames"); 7th-rank rook pawn vs Q (Practice "Pawn endgames") | Weak squares, open files, good/bad bishop, IQP basics | Repertoire to ~10 moves plus one plan per line | Converting advantages; Heisman's process-error list in review |
| **3: 1800–2000** | `quietMove`, `defensiveMove`, `interference`, `zugzwang`, `sacrifice`, `exposedKing`, `kingsideAttack`, `veryLong` | Candidates and comparison; the opponent's resources/prophylaxis (Dvoretsky, *Recognizing Your Opponent's Resources*) | Practical rook endings with several pawns, B vs N, opposite bishops, outside passer | Structures (Carlsbad, IQP, hanging pawns); exchanges, incl. the queen trade (Dvoretsky) | Typical middlegames of your openings | Defending worse positions (Aimchess's "resourcefulness"); clock |
| **4: 2000–2200** | Mixed sets with no theme shown (Steps' "mixed" block); `underPromotion`, long combinations | Long forcing lines; "is there a tactic at all?" (Dojo: not every test has one) | Corresponding squares, rook endings in depth, Q endings basics | Prophylaxis, dynamic vs static, positional sacrifices | Deeper, own analysis of critical lines | Tournament-style prep; mistakes by phase and clock |

**Where the practice material comes from**

- *Tactics, calculation, mates:* Lichess puzzle DB (CC0), filtered by `Themes`, `Rating` (Glicko-2) and `Popularity`. Note that the first UCI move in `Moves` is the opponent's move and the puzzle starts after it (the tagger sets `pov = not game.turn()`, [model.py](https://github.com/ornicar/lichess-puzzler/blob/master/tagger/model.py) [primary]). Puzzles always have a solution; "no-tactic" items for Dojo-style tests need positions from the evaluations DB or the games DB where Stockfish shows no winning shot.
- *Endgame play-outs:* the games DB (CC0) filtered by material signature, e.g. KRPvKR; Lichess Practice positions are textbook classics and should not be copied. Adjudicate by tablebase.
- *Strategy/structure play-outs:* games-DB positions found by a pawn-structure detector (e.g. an IQP), played out against Maia and scored by the Stockfish eval change. This adjudication is fuzzy, so it is weaker than endgames as a pass test.
- *Openings:* the existing repertoire + the CC0 opening names already shipped, with frequency stats precomputed from the games DB (the explorer API now needs a per-user token, see §4).
- *Own games:* existing Stockfish review → mistake positions → routed by motif (tagger, §4) or by material/phase.
- *Theory pointers:* names only ("Lucena position", "Philidor defence", Lichess Practice chapter, Silman's class part), no copied text.

**What the licences mean here** (not legal advice). P-Chess's repo is public with **no licence file** ([spec.md](../spec.md)), so legally all rights are reserved. Shipping GPL programs (Stockfish, lc0) and GPL data (Maia-1) *beside* it as separate files is "mere aggregation", which the GPL allows ([GPL FAQ](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/gpl-faq.html#MereAggregation)). **Porting or linking GPL/AGPL code into P-Chess itself** (e.g. translating `cook.py` to GDScript) makes P-Chess a derivative work that must be released under GPL-3.0/AGPL-3.0. AGPL's extra clause concerns network services, so for a desktop app it behaves almost like GPL-3.0. Options: (a) run the Python tagger as a separate process and ship its source; (b) write an independent detector from the public theme *definitions* (the risk is a close translation; ideas are not copyrightable, code is); (c) license P-Chess GPL-3.0/AGPL-3.0 and port it. The sound files already shipped are AGPL-3.0 (THIRD_PARTY.md), so (c) is the least friction.

**Can the tagger tag your own mistakes?** Partly. How it works [primary, read from code]: `tagger/cook.py` (~1,000 lines, Python + python-chess) takes a `Puzzle`: a game rooted at the position, `mainline[0]` = the opponent's move that creates the puzzle, then the forced solution line, plus the eval in cp. It runs ~50 rule-based detectors (58 tag kinds in `model.py`) over the solver's moves (`mainline[1::2]`). For example, `fork` = the moved piece, not itself en prise, attacks ≥2 non-pawn pieces that are more valuable or hanging. It also adds eval bands (`crushing` >600 cp, `advantage` >200, else `equality`), length tags, and phase tags from material (`rookEndgame` etc.). `zugzwang` needs an engine call ([cook.py](https://github.com/ornicar/lichess-puzzler/blob/master/tagger/cook.py), [model.py](https://github.com/ornicar/lichess-puzzler/blob/master/tagger/model.py)). The generator only accepts lines where the best move's win chance beats the second-best by >0.7, i.e. "only moves" ([generator.py](https://github.com/ornicar/lichess-puzzler/blob/master/generator/generator.py)).
→ For a game mistake: feed it [your opponent's previous move + Stockfish's PV from the position where you erred]. It tags the motif of **what you missed**, as long as that PV is a forcing tactic. Quiet or positional mistakes come back with only phase/eval tags, and a non-only-move PV gives noisier tags. It cannot say *why* you missed it; Heisman's process-error list in the guided review covers that.

## 5. What makes self-guided practice feel like progress

- **Gate units on mastery, not completion.** Mastery-learning programmes improved exam scores across 108 controlled evaluations, with larger effects reported for weaker students [secondary] (Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns 1990, [abs](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543060002265); the ~0.5 SD figure is secondary). Khan Academy's public rules are a usable model: Familiar → Proficient → Mastered, with Mastered only from a unit test, and demotion on later failure ([primary help](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/115002552631); the 2018 demotion rules may be stale). Dojo deliberately does the opposite: it is ungated, and rating gain graduates you. Both are defensible; P-Chess can gate units while letting rating unlock stages.
- **Keep success near ~85% while practising.** The "Eighty Five Percent Rule" is derived for gradient-learning binary classifiers and models, not for chess or humans ([Wilson et al. 2019, primary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579)); use it as a tuning target for puzzle difficulty, not as a fact. Lichess puzzle Glicko-2 ratings make this tunable.
- **Placement test:** a short timed set across themes with Glicko-style rating (Dojo Tactics Tests; Noctie's free "rating test", [primary](https://noctie.ai/pricing)), then start one stage below the weakest area.
- **Progress the user can see:** a per-theme rating (Lichess already shows a per-theme puzzle dashboard), play-out score against Maia at the stage's top rating, and the pass tests passed. Rating change will lag and cannot be attributed to the app (chess-learning.md §3.5), so don't make it the headline metric.
- **Habit mechanics:** Duolingo reports 7-day-streak learners are 2.4× more likely to return the next day, and that decoupling the streak from the daily goal raised streak-holding new learners by 19% ([primary blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)). That is retention, not learning, so use it sparingly. Dojo's community accountability ("plus, equal, minus") cannot be copied offline; a weekly self-report screen is the cheap substitute.
- **Interleave after blocking:** teach a unit blocked, then mix its positions into later units' sets without a theme label (Steps' mixed block; interleaving evidence in chess-learning.md).

## 6. Recommendation: the first vertical slice

**Scoring candidate themes on four constraints** (puzzle counts are all-rating totals from the [Lichess themes page](https://lichess.org/training/themes) today [primary]; I did not count per rating band):

| Theme | (1) Route own mistakes without the AGPL tagger? | (2) Puzzle supply in band | (3) Play-out result decidable? | (4) Objective pass test? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Rook endgames (Lucena/Philidor → practical)** | **Yes**: material signature only (KR(P)vKR) | `rookEndgame` 341,661; these are *tactics inside* rook endings, not Lucena/Philidor technique, so technique comes from the play-outs | **Yes, exactly**: ≤5 pieces → tablebase | **Yes**: hold/win from N positions against Maia, plus a puzzle score |
| Hanging pieces / blunder check | Partly: "a piece was en prise after your move" is a cheap check; full motif tags need the tagger | `hangingPiece` 256,198 | Weak: after the tactic there is little to play out | Yes, as puzzles only |
| Forks | Needs the tagger (or a re-implementation) | `fork` 849,976 | Weak | Yes, as puzzles only |

**Build: "Stage 2 · Unit: Rook endgames I (Lucena & Philidor)".** One unit, end to end:
1. *Theory pointer:* "Read about the Lucena position and the Philidor defence" (Lichess Practice "Rook endgames" or any endgame manual). No text copied.
2. *Solving set:* ~20 `rookEndgame` puzzles in the user's band (tactics inside rook endings), "calculate, then commit": enter the line including the defender's replies (Dojo format), with the engine answer shown right after.
3. *Play-outs:* 5–10 KRPvKR positions mined from the CC0 games DB, played against Maia-1 at 1500 → 1700 → 1900, as both the winning and the defending side. Tablebase scores each as pass/fail.
4. *Pass test:* e.g. ≥80% of play-outs held/converted at the top Maia level and ≥70% of a fresh puzzle set. These thresholds are guesses to tune.
5. *Routing:* the existing review flags any game mistake in a KR(P)vKR position and offers the unit. The annotate-before-engine step goes in here.

It sits in Stage 2, one stage below Bruno (Stage 3), so for him it doubles as a placement check. Why this one: it exercises every new piece (unit model, solving UI, Maia play-out from a FEN, adjudication, pass test, routing from your own games) without touching the tagger licence question; adjudication is exact rather than an eval guess; Bruno's band (Lichess ~1900 ≈ Silman/Dojo ~Class B/A) is where these endings are standard material; and it reuses the Maia bots and review board already in the app.

**Main risks**
- **Licence:** any tagger reuse forces a P-Chess licence decision (§4); the slice avoids it, and the second unit (tactics) won't.
- **Maia ceiling:** Maia-1 stops at 1900, so "rising levels" cap exactly at Bruno's rating. Going higher means integrating Maia-3 (AGPL, PyTorch, a separate engine process; size and CPU speed not measured).
- **Maia in technical endgames:** Maia plays without search and is trained to be human, so it may defend KRPvKR badly or oddly. That is realistic, but a pass may then be too easy. Check by playing a few positions first; if needed, mix in Stockfish-at-low-depth defence (not tested).
- **Tablebase delivery:** bundling ~1 GB versus calling the Lichess API (online; anonymous today, could change like the explorer did).
- **Content load:** each unit needs curated positions, thresholds and pointers; Dojo needed 18 months and three titled coaches to agree on a universal plan.
- **No measurable rating effect** (chess-learning.md). Success has to be judged on per-theme ratings and pass rates, not Elo.
- **Competition:** Noctie covers much of the pitch commercially; P-Chess's case rests on stages + gating + free/offline.
